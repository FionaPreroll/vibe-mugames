# The games

The Spielekiste's games and their rules, in short. In the Spielekiste, each game's **?** in the chooser, or **Rules** beside its name in a game, shows its rules a step at a time, with drawings.

## Tic Tac Toe

3 by 3 squares. In turn, each player marks a free square. Three in a row, across, down or diagonally, win; a full board without one is a draw.

## Ultimate Tic Tac Toe

Nine boards of Tic Tac Toe, laid out as one big board of 3 by 3. In turn, each player marks a free square of a small board. The square marked sends the other player to the small board in the same place of the big one: a mark in a small board's top right square sends them to the top right board, which the board marks. Three in a row win a small board; a full one without is nobody's. A board that is won or full takes no more marks, and a player sent there may mark any open board. Three small boards won in a row, across, down or diagonally, win the game; once every board is closed without one, it is a draw.

## Four in a Row

7 columns of 6 squares. In turn, each player drops a piece into a column, where it falls to the lowest free square. Four in a row, across, down or diagonally, win; a full board without one is a draw.

## Nine Men's Morris

Each player places nine pieces, one a turn, then moves them along a line to a free neighbouring point; with only three left, they jump to any free point. Three in a row on a line make a mill, which takes one of the other player's pieces, one outside a mill if there is any. Fewer than three pieces, or none that can move, lose; fifty moves without a mill are a draw.

## Draughts

Twelve pieces each on the dark squares of 8 by 8. Pieces step diagonally forward and take by jumping, forward and backward, on and on while they can. Taking is a must, but any way to take may be chosen. A piece that ends its move on the far row is crowned and then moves and takes at any distance. A player who cannot move loses; fifty moves of crowned pieces without taking are a draw.

## Ludo

Four pieces each, in a corner of the board, and a track of 40 squares round it. On their turn, a player rolls the die and moves one of their pieces that many squares on; a six brings a piece out of the corner onto its start square, and rolls again. A piece that lands on another player's piece sends it back to its corner; two pieces of one player never share a square. After a round, a piece goes into its four home squares, by the exact number. A player with no piece on the track rolls up to three times for a six; when no piece can move, the turn passes on. Whoever has all four pieces home first wins. Tap a piece to see where it goes, then tap there. Each phone shows its own corner at the bottom left; two players sit in opposite corners.

## Miau-Miau

Five cards each, from 32 cards (seven to ace) for up to four players, or 52 for five and six. A card goes on the pile if it has the suit or the rank of the top card; otherwise, or by choice, the player draws one, and may play it at once if it fits. A seven makes the next player draw two, unless they answer with a seven, which passes four on, and so on. An eight makes the next player miss their turn. A jack goes on any card and wishes for a suit. The first to play their last card wins. Each phone sees only its own hand; a card help on the screen explains the cards.

## Sea Battle

Each player places a fleet on a sea of 10 by 10: one ship of five squares, one of four, two of three and one of two, across or down, none touching another, not even at a corner. The fleet starts shuffled; tap a ship to pick it, tap it again to turn it, tap the sea to move it there, or shuffle again, then **Ready**. Both place at the same time, then they shoot at each other's sea in turns, a square a shot: a hit shoots again, a miss passes the turn. Once every square of a ship is hit, it is sunk, and the squares around it are marked as water. Whoever sinks the whole other fleet first wins. Each phone sees only its own fleet, and every fleet once the game is over.

## Mäxchen

A dice game of bluffing, with three lives each. The player to move rolls two dice under the cup, looks at them alone, and says what they show, the truth or a bluff, but always more than the player before said. Two dice are read with the higher first, so a 5 and a 3 are 53; doubles beat every other throw, and the Mäxchen, 2-1, beats everything: 31 is the lowest. The next player believes it and rolls in turn, or doubts it and lifts the cup: if it holds less than was said, the one who said it loses a life, otherwise the one who doubted does. Against a Mäxchen, a player gives up a life, or doubts it, which costs two if it is true. Whoever lost starts the next round; the last one with a life left wins. Only the roller's phone shows the dice; the game says in how many of 36 throws two dice reach what was said.

## Werewolf

A village of four to eight, with werewolves hidden in it: one for four and five players, two from six, a seer always and a healer from five; everyone else is a villager. Each phone shows only its own role, and the werewolves see each other. At night, everyone taps someone in secret: the werewolves a victim (chance decides if they disagree), the seer whom to learn about, the healer whom to protect, themselves too but not the same twice in a row, and villagers whom they suspect, which changes nothing but gives no role away. In the morning, everyone learns who was taken, and what they were. By day, the village talks, then everyone still in votes: whoever gets the most votes is out and their role shown, and a tie banishes nobody. The village wins once every werewolf is out, the werewolves once they are as many as the others; a win counts for the whole team. Werewolf is a party game anyone may play; the Spielekiste's roles and words are its own.

## City, Country, River

_Stadt, Land, Fluss_, for two to eight. Each round has a letter, drawn by chance, a new one every round (never C, Q, X or Y). Everyone writes, on their own phone, a word starting with it for each category: a city, a country, a river, a first name, an animal and a job; Ä counts as A. Nobody sees another's answers while they write. Whoever has a word in every category may call **Stop!**: the others have ten seconds left. After three minutes, the round ends anyway.

Then everyone sees every answer, and taps those of the others that do not count, such as a river that does not exist: an answer that more than half of the others object to counts nothing, and so does one that does not start with the letter. An answer counts 20 if nobody else has one in its category, 10 if nobody else has the same word, and 5 if someone does. Once everyone has tapped **Next round**, the next letter comes. After five rounds, the most points win; with the same, it is a draw.

On one device, everyone writes in turn, as long as they like, while the others look away, and taps **Done**; then the answers are checked together, and one tap strikes an answer.

## Light Racers

A game in real time. Every rider races across a grid of 40 by 40, ten squares a second, and leaves a wall of light behind. Riding into a wall, the edge or another rider puts a rider out; the last one riding wins, and riders who crash at the same moment as the last others make a draw. Turn by swiping on the field, or with **⟲** and **⟳**, or the arrow keys; riders cannot turn straight back. A ring shows each phone its rider during the countdown.

## Air Hockey

A game in real time, for two. Each phone shows the whole rink from its player's end, with their goal at the bottom. Under _Settings_, _Show the whole rink_ can be switched off: then each screen is its player's half alone, and with both phones laid on the table, top edge to top edge, the puck slides from one screen to the other. _Smooth out the network_, on unless switched off, draws the puck and the other mallet at an even pace, however unevenly the host's states arrive. The small graph of the ping shows beside the score, as the rink covers the top line. A finger moves the mallet, which follows it quickly but not at once; your phone draws it so straight away, without waiting for the host's answer. A puck in a goal is a point for the other player, who gets the puck back on their side; the first to seven goals wins. Anyone watching sees the whole rink. _Pong_ is a trademark, so the Spielekiste plays air hockey, a game anyone may make.

## Dots and Boxes

6 by 6 dots, which make 5 by 5 boxes. In turn, each player draws a line between two neighbouring dots, across or down. Whoever draws the fourth side of a box wins it and draws again; a line that closes no box passes the turn. Once every line is drawn, whoever holds more boxes wins.

## Reversi

8 by 8 squares, two pieces each crosswise in the middle to start. A piece goes where it encloses one or more lines of the other's pieces, across, down or diagonally, between it and one of the player's own: those all turn. A player who cannot enclose anything passes; once neither can, whoever has more pieces wins. The board marks where a piece may go. _Othello_ is a trademark, so the Spielekiste plays Reversi, its older name.

## Chinese Checkers

A star of 121 holes, for two to six, each with ten pieces in a triangle of the star, across from another player's or from an empty one. A move is a step to a free neighbouring hole, or jumps, each over one neighbouring piece, anyone's, to the free hole behind it, as many in a row as the player likes; nothing is taken. The first to fill the triangle across with their pieces wins; a piece of another player that never left its own triangle there does not spoil it. A player who cannot move passes, and thirty rounds in which no piece reaches its triangle are a draw. Tap a piece, then where it goes: the board finds the way there. Each phone turns the star so that its own triangle is at the bottom, and rings each player's goal in their colour.

## Choosing a game

The chooser sorts the games by kind: _Tactics_ (the board games, with nothing left to chance), _Luck and bluff_ (dice, cards and something hidden) and _Skill_ (the games in real time); each game shows how many play it. Each game in the chooser shows a picture of its board, and its **?** opens its rules, a step at a time with drawings; in a game, **Rules** beside its name does the same. Players can read them while the host chooses.

To make a move of several steps, tap the piece, then where it goes, and every square a jump lands on.
